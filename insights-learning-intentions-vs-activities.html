Learning Intention or Activity? A Quick Test for Teachers

One of the most common slips in lesson planning is writing an activity where a learning intention should be. It is an easy mistake to make, and an easy one to fix once you know what to look for.

The test

Read your target and ask: "If a student did this perfectly, would they necessarily have learned something?"

Students can complete a worksheet, watch a video, or finish a group project without learning what you intended. A true learning intention names the understanding or skill itself. An activity names the task.

Before and after

Activity (what students do)Learning intention (what students learn)
Complete page 42 of the workbook.I can add fractions with unlike denominators by finding a common denominator.
Read chapter 3 and answer the questions.I can explain how the author uses setting to build tension.
Make a poster about the water cycle.I can describe the stages of the water cycle and what drives each one.
Do the lab on density.I can predict whether an object will float by comparing its density to the liquid's.

Three steps to rewrite any target

  1. Remove the task verb. Words like complete, read, watch, make, and do usually signal an activity.
  2. Ask what you want students to understand or be able to do afterward. Use verbs like explain, compare, solve, predict, or justify.
  3. Say it in student language. "I can..." statements work well because students can check themselves against them.

Then add success criteria

A learning intention says where students are headed. Success criteria say what it looks like when they arrive. For the fractions example: "I find a common denominator correctly. I rewrite both fractions. I add the numerators and simplify the answer."

One more check

Before the lesson ends, ask a student: "What are you learning today?" If the answer describes the activity, the target may need another look. That feedback is useful for any teacher, and most improve quickly with a little practice.
