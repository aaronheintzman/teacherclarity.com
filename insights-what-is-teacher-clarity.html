What Is Teacher Clarity? A Plain-Language Guide

Teacher clarity sounds like a simple idea, and it is. It means that students can answer three questions at any point in a lesson: What am I learning? Why does it matter? How will I know when I have got it?

The three parts

Learning intentions

A learning intention states what students will learn, not what they will do. "Compare how two authors build suspense" is a learning intention. "Read chapters 3 and 4" is an activity.

Success criteria

Success criteria describe what good work looks like, in language students can use. They let students check their own progress instead of waiting for a grade.

Clear feedback

When intentions and criteria are clear, feedback can be specific: here is where your work meets the criteria, and here is the next step.

Why it matters

In John Hattie's Visible Learning® research, a synthesis of over 2,100 meta-analyses, teacher clarity has an effect size of 0.85. Hattie's hinge point for an influence worth prioritizing is 0.4, roughly a year of growth for a year of schooling. At 0.85, teacher clarity more than doubles that benchmark.

That result is not about a special program or a purchase. It is about a habit of teaching: making the target visible so students can aim at it.

What it looks like in a classroom

  • A learning intention is stated in student-friendly language and revisited during the lesson.
  • Students can explain, in their own words, what they are working toward.
  • Success criteria are visible, and students use them to improve their work.
  • The activities clearly connect to the learning, not just to filling time.

Common mistakes

  • Posting a target and never using it. A learning intention on the board that nobody refers to does little.
  • Writing activities as intentions. "Complete the worksheet" tells students what to do, not what to learn.
  • Making criteria a checklist of compliance. Criteria should describe quality, not just completion.

Where to start

Pick one lesson this week. Write the learning intention in one sentence a student could repeat. Add two or three success criteria. Then ask students, partway through, what they are learning and how they will know they have got it. Their answers will tell you quickly how clear the lesson really is.
